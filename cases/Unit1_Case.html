Lumen Labs: Three Ideas, One Team

A three-person founding team in Munich must choose what to build
©️ Dr. Hildegard Haas · Genedia AG Education
Case Study

Lumen Labs is not yet a company. It is three people who met during a master's programme in Munich and who have agreed to give one idea eighteen months of their lives. Nadia is a mechanical engineer who spent four years at a lighting manufacturer. Piotr writes software and has shipped two small products alone. Elif worked in procurement for a hospital group and knows how public buyers behave.

They have three candidate ideas on the table. The first is a retrofit sensor that makes existing office lighting responsive to occupancy, sold to facility managers. The second is a consumer app that scores the light quality of a room and recommends changes, monetised through affiliate sales. The third is a compliance service for hospitals that documents lighting conditions against European workplace standards — a market Elif knows in detail and which nobody in the team finds exciting.

Nadia is convinced by the first idea because she can build it. Piotr prefers the second because it can be launched in six weeks and tested cheaply with strangers. Elif argues, without enthusiasm, for the third: she can name eleven potential buyers and describe how they budget.

They have run all three ideas through a generative AI tool, which produced encouraging market sizing for each — a €4.1 billion European market for the first, €900 million for the second, and no figure at all for the third because the category does not exist as a named market. The team found the first number persuasive. None of them could locate the source when they tried.

Their combined savings are €62,000. Two of the three can work unpaid for a year; Piotr cannot go beyond eight months. There is a regional grant available, but the application requires a named customer segment and a stated problem, submitted in six weeks.

Your task

  1. Screen all three ideas with the NABC Idea Screen. Which survives, and what did you have to assume to keep it alive?
  2. Apply the Timmons Model. For each idea, which of the three forces — opportunity, resources, team — is weakest?
  3. Rewrite the team's decision effectually: starting from who they are, what they know and whom they know, which idea does the bird-in-hand principle favour, and why?
  4. The €4.1 billion figure could not be traced to a source. What should the team do with it, and what does the Source Triangulation Check require here?
  5. Elif's idea is the one nobody finds exciting. Does that matter? Argue both sides using Schumpeter and Sarasvathy.
Bring written answers to questions 1 and 2; the remaining questions are worked in the session.

Lumen Labs is a teaching case constructed for MENTR101. The situation is composite; no real company is described.
