Nimbus Fleet: Selling the Van or the Kilometre

A Rotterdam venture with a working product and an unresolved revenue model
©️ Dr. Hildegard Haas · Genedia AG Education
Case Study
A working product is not a business; the model is how the value you create comes back as money.Core message after Bostelaar · Intended learning outcomes 1, 3

Nimbus Fleet builds a driver-assistance retrofit for small commercial van fleets: a camera unit and software that reduces insurance-relevant incidents. The hardware works. Three pilot fleets in the Netherlands and Belgium have run it for nine months, and incident rates fell by a reported eighteen per cent.

The founders cannot agree on how to charge. The first option is a one-off hardware sale at €890 per van with an optional €12 monthly software fee. The second is a full subscription at €39 per van per month with the hardware included. The third, favoured by their newest advisor, is a share of the insurance premium reduction the fleet achieves — no fixed fee at all.

The economics differ sharply. The hardware costs €410 to produce and install. Sales currently take eleven weeks from first contact to signature and consume roughly €2,300 of founder time and travel per fleet. Fleets average forty vans. The pilots renewed, but two of the three negotiated the price down at renewal.

The software improves as it accumulates driving data across fleets, and the founders describe this in their deck as a data network effect. Pressed by an investor, they could not name the user-visible improvement that results, nor the point at which a fleet would notice it.

Their AI cost line in the financial model is a single figure: €600 per month for inference. There is no line for data labelling, no line for the engineer who retrains the model quarterly, and no line for the human review of flagged incidents, which one founder currently does on evenings and weekends.

A large insurer has offered a distribution partnership on the condition that Nimbus adopts the premium-share model. It would multiply reach and remove the founders' control over pricing.

Your task

  1. Complete a Business Model Canvas for each of the three pricing options. Which blocks change, and which stay the same?
  2. Calculate CAC, contribution margin and payback for the one-off model and the subscription model. Which one survives a bad quarter?
  3. Cost the AI Iceberg properly. What is missing from the €600, and what does that do to the unit economics?
  4. Is the data network effect claim justified? Apply the flywheel framework and identify the missing link.
  5. Should Nimbus accept the insurer partnership? Use Build, Buy or Partner and the Automation-Augmentation Paradox in your answer.
Bring written answers to questions 1 and 2; the remaining questions are worked in the session.

Nimbus Fleet is a teaching case constructed for MENTR101. The situation is composite; no real company is described.
