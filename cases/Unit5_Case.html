Aurora Bikes: Ninety Days of Runway

A Lisbon hardware venture decides how to survive its own growth
©️ Dr. Hildegard Haas · Genedia AG Education
Case Study
Cash is a date, not a number — know the date, and know what moves it.Core message after Bostelaar · Intended learning outcomes 3, 4

Aurora Bikes assembles cargo e-bikes for urban delivery businesses in Lisbon and Porto. Revenue has grown every quarter for two years and reached €1.9 million in the last twelve months. The company has never been profitable and now has ninety days of cash.

The pattern is familiar and avoidable. Each bike sells for €4,200 and costs €2,750 to build. Customers pay thirty per cent on order and the balance on delivery; suppliers require payment on shipment, ninety days before delivery. Every additional order therefore consumes cash before it produces any. Growth is the problem, not the solution.

The founder's financial model shows profit in month fourteen. It is a single spreadsheet tab in which the income statement is calculated but no balance sheet exists and cash flow is a row of monthly profit. Working capital does not appear anywhere. Asked why cash is short when the business is profitable on paper, the founder had no answer.

Three routes are open. A bank has offered a €300,000 working capital facility at nine per cent, secured personally. An angel group has offered €600,000 for twenty-five per cent, with a liquidation preference and a €150,000 option pool carved out of existing shareholders. A supplier has offered ninety-day payment terms in exchange for a two per cent price increase and a two-year exclusive.

The team has also modelled a price rise. At €4,600 the sales lead believes conversion falls by roughly a fifth; nobody has tested this. Break-even volume at the current price is 41 bikes per month; the company is selling 38.

One further complication: the founder pasted the full customer list and pricing into a public AI assistant while asking it to build a cash flow projection.

Your task

  1. Explain, using the Three-Statement Model, why a profitable business has run out of cash. Which statement was missing?
  2. Compute break-even at €4,200 and at €4,600 under the assumed conversion drop. Which price survives a bad quarter?
  3. Evaluate the three financing routes on cost, control and speed. Model the dilution of the angel offer including the pool.
  4. Build base, best and worst cases. Is the worst case survivable, and what early warning indicator would you set?
  5. The customer list was pasted into a public AI tool. Apply the Confidentiality Tiers framework: what has happened, and what must the founder do now?
Bring written answers to questions 1 and 2; the remaining questions are worked in the session.

Aurora Bikes is a teaching case constructed for MENTR101. The situation is composite; no real company is described.
