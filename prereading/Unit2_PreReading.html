The Interview That Lies to You

Why validation evidence is usually manufactured by the person collecting it
©️ Dr. Hildegard Haas · Genedia AG Education
Pre-Reading
Key framework
The Mom Test · Jobs to Be Done

Customer discovery has a structural problem: the person collecting the evidence wants a particular answer, and the person giving it wants to be kind. The result is a conversation that feels like validation and contains none.

Fitzpatrick (2013) named the mechanism precisely. Ask anyone whether your idea is good and they will protect your feelings. Ask about a hypothetical future — would you use this, would you pay — and they will answer with an intention, which is free to give and predicts nothing. The only reliable material is what a person has already done, what it cost them, and what they have already tried to fix it.

Blank's customer development process (2012) puts this discipline inside a structure. A start-up, in his formulation, is a temporary organisation searching for a repeatable business model, and discovery is the phase in which the model's assumptions are converted into hypotheses and tested with people who are not you. The output of discovery is not a validated idea; it is a smaller number of surviving assumptions.

Christensen's Jobs to Be Done adds the missing question. Customers do not buy products; they hire something to make progress in a circumstance. If you do not know the circumstance, you will misidentify the competition — and the most common unlisted competitor is not another company but the workaround the customer already has and the option of doing nothing at all.

Two failure modes are worth naming before you begin. The first is the compliment collected as data. The second is the market figure that arrives without a source — increasingly from an AI assistant, which will supply a plausible number for any market you name. Neither survives an investor conversation, and neither survives the marking criteria for Assessment 1.

What does survive is a small quantity of specific, awkward evidence: three people who described the same problem in their own words, one of whom paid you something to solve it.

Validation is not the number of people who liked your idea; it is the number of assumptions your evidence failed to kill.

Questions to bring to the session

  1. What is the last thing you personally paid to solve, and what did you try before paying?
  2. Which of your venture's assumptions would be most expensive to be wrong about?
  3. Who is your real competitor if the customer simply does nothing?

References

Fitzpatrick, R. (2013) The Mom Test. London: Robfitz Ltd.
Blank, S. and Dorf, B. (2012) The Startup Owner's Manual. Pescadero: K&S Ranch Press.
