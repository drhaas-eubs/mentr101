A Business Model Is Not a Product

Why ventures with working products still fail to capture value
©️ Dr. Hildegard Haas · Genedia AG Education
Pre-Reading
A working product is not a business; the model is how the value you create comes back as money.Core message after Bostelaar · Intended learning outcomes 1, 3
Key framework
Business Model Canvas · Unit Economics

A product is what you make. A business model is the logic by which making it produces more value than it consumes. Ventures fail at the second while succeeding at the first more often than founders expect, and the failure is usually invisible until the money runs out.

Osterwalder and Pigneur (2010) gave the concept a shared vocabulary: nine blocks that can be drawn, compared and argued about. The canvas's value is that it makes the model a manipulable object. Change the customer segment and watch what happens to channels, relationships and cost. Change the revenue mechanism and watch the whole cash profile move.

But a canvas describes; it does not test. This is the most common misuse of the tool in student work and in practice. A complete, confident, entirely fictional canvas is easy to produce in twenty minutes — and easier still with AI assistance. The discipline lies in marking which blocks rest on evidence from real customers and which rest on assumption.

The economics live in two places the canvas only gestures at. The first is the revenue mechanism: a one-off sale, a subscription and a usage fee are not three prices for the same thing but three different businesses, with different cash timing, different churn exposure and different forecasting methods. The second is unit economics — what one customer costs to acquire, what one customer contributes, and how long the recovery takes.

Where AI is part of the model, two further disciplines apply. Cost it honestly: the visible licence or inference fee is the smallest part of the total, and the submerged costs — data preparation, integration, monitoring, human review — are permanent, not one-off. And test the defensibility claim: a data flywheel only turns if the improvement it produces is visible to the user, and most claimed flywheels have no such link.

The question to bring to the session is unglamorous and decisive: who pays, for what, on what schedule, and does the money arriving exceed the money leaving before the money runs out?

A business model is only as strong as its weakest evidenced block and its unit economics; everything else on the canvas is a hypothesis.

Questions to bring to the session

  1. Which block of your canvas has the least evidence behind it, and what would the cheapest test of it be?
  2. If you doubled your price tomorrow, what would break first — demand, delivery or trust?
  3. What is the submerged cost in your model that nobody has yet put a number on?
