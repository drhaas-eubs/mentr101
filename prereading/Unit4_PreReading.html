Plans Are Useless, Planning Is Indispensable

What a business plan is actually for, and why fluent plans fail hardest
©️ Dr. Hildegard Haas · Genedia AG Education
Pre-Reading
Key framework
Minto Pyramid Principle · Risk Matrix

Eisenhower's remark about planning is quoted in every strategy course and understood in almost none. The point is not that plans are worthless but that their value lies in the reasoning they force, not in the document they produce. A plan that was never argued about is decoration.

The reader of a business plan has one question: what must be true for this to work? Every section answers a version of it. The market analysis answers whether the demand exists; the operational plan whether you can deliver; the financial plan whether the arithmetic survives; the risk section whether you have looked honestly at what could end it.

Structure determines whether that argument is visible. Minto's (1987) pyramid inverts the writer's discovery order: the governing answer first, then grouped supporting arguments, then evidence. A reader who stops after two sentences should still know what you are proposing and why. Most student plans, and most founder plans, bury the answer on page two.

The risk section is where maturity shows. A list of five generic risks with one-line mitigations tells a reader that the team has not thought about failure. ISO 31000 asks for something harder: identify, analyse, evaluate, treat — with an owner, a trigger and a written response for anything both likely and consequential. Naming a real risk is a signal of competence, not of weakness.

Generative AI has made this harder rather than easier, in one specific way. It produces plans that read as though the reasoning happened. Fluency now costs nothing, which means fluency no longer signals anything, and the reader compensates by testing the underlying claims harder. Two consequences follow: every fact must be verified at its source, and every citation must be opened — fabricated references are the most common and most damaging failure in AI-assisted writing.

Use the tools. Draft with them, and especially critique with them: asking a model to attack your own plan against the marking criteria is one of the most useful things it can do. Then verify, cite, and declare. The plan is yours; so is the responsibility for every number in it.

A plan earns credibility through structure, specific risk and verified evidence — not through fluent prose, which is now free.

Questions to bring to the session

  1. What is the one sentence in your plan that, if false, ends the venture?
  2. Which risk have you left out because naming it would be uncomfortable?
  3. If a reader stopped after your first two sentences, what would they know?
