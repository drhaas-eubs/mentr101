You Cannot Patent an Idea

What intellectual property actually protects, and what founders lose by default
©️ Dr. Hildegard Haas · Genedia AG Education
Pre-Reading
What you have not deliberately protected, you have already given away.Core message after Bostelaar · Intended learning outcomes 3, 4
Key framework
IP Protection Matrix · Patentability Filter

Founders talk about protecting the idea. No legal system anywhere protects ideas. What the regimes protect are specific things: a technical invention, a sign that identifies commercial origin, an expression fixed in a form, an appearance, and information kept genuinely secret. Each has different requirements, different durations and different ways of being destroyed.

The most expensive mistakes in early ventures are not failures to file. They are irreversible acts taken in ignorance. A patent requires novelty, and novelty is destroyed by any public disclosure before filing — including a pitch, a demo day, a trade interview or a detailed LinkedIn post. A trade secret requires secrecy and documented protective measures; publishing the method or letting staff leave without agreements ends it. A logo drawn by a contractor belongs to the contractor unless the contract says otherwise.

Trade marks add a further trap. Protection is territorial and granted only for specified classes of goods and services. Using a name first is not the same as owning it, and discovering a prior EU registration after two years of brand building is a common and entirely avoidable event: a clearance search takes an afternoon.

Operations carry their own structural questions. Legal form determines personal liability, taxation and whether investors can hold shares at all — cheap to choose, expensive to change. Dependencies determine whether a single supplier, a single platform or a single API can stop the business; efficiency-optimised chains are brittle, and platform dependency is a supply chain risk even when nothing physical moves.

Compliance now sits inside this same design conversation rather than after it. The EU AI Act assigns duties by risk tier and, crucially, places obligations on the deployer of an AI system as well as on the provider — most ventures buy AI rather than build it, and buying does not transfer accountability. The GDPR requires a lawful basis identified and documented before personal data is processed, not afterwards.

None of this is glamorous, and all of it is decided by default if you do not decide it deliberately. The default is rarely in your favour.

Protection follows the regime, not the intention: name the asset, choose the regime, and know the act that would destroy it.

Questions to bring to the session

  1. What has your venture already disclosed publicly that cannot now be patented?
  2. Who legally owns your logo, your code and your content — and where is that written down?
  3. If your venture deploys AI, are you a provider, a deployer, or both?
