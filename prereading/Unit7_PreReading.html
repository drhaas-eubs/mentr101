The Cheapest Customer Is the One Who Stays

Why acquisition metrics flatter ventures that are quietly failing
©️ Dr. Hildegard Haas · Genedia AG Education
Pre-Reading
Key framework
AARRR Pirate Metrics · Cohort Retention

Acquisition is visible, purchasable and easy to celebrate. Activation and retention are neither. This asymmetry explains most of the misdirected effort in early-stage marketing: teams optimise the number they can move with money and ignore the number that determines whether the money was worth spending.

McClure's AARRR framework exists to correct the imbalance. Five stages — acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue — each with its own conversion rate, and only one of them worth working on at any moment. The bottleneck is almost never acquisition. A venture that buys traffic before activation works is converting cash into registrations.

Cohort analysis makes the failure visible. Group customers by the month they joined and track whether they are still active. A curve that flattens indicates a returning core; a curve that reaches zero indicates that every euro of acquisition spend has to be spent again. Aggregate user counts conceal both, which is why they appear so often in decks.

Positioning is the upstream cause of most retention problems. Segmentation, targeting and positioning are usually taught as a marketing sequence; in practice they are a decision about whom to exclude. Attracting users you cannot serve — because of language, regulation, price or fit — produces exactly the pattern in this unit's case: rising traffic, falling quality, and a cost per real customer nobody has calculated.

AI has amplified both the opportunity and the failure mode. Content, targeting and personalisation at scale are now available to a two-person team. The constraints are unchanged and now more consequential: personalisation requires a lawful basis for the data it uses, and data belonging to your customers' customers is not yours to use. Relevance and intrusion are separated by consent, not by intention.

One final discipline, from Eyal's own manipulation matrix: engagement mechanics that serve the metric rather than the user are a business risk, not a growth tactic. Design what you would be willing to explain to the user.

Measure to activation and retention, not to traffic; and never personalise with data you have no basis to use.

Questions to bring to the session

  1. Which stage of your funnel is genuinely the weakest, and how do you know?
  2. What would your cost per retained customer be, as opposed to your cost per signup?
  3. Which personalisation would you be uncomfortable explaining to the customer?
