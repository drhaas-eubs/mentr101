Scaling Is a Decision, Not a Reward

What breaks when a venture grows, and why it breaks predictably
©️ Dr. Hildegard Haas · Genedia AG Education
Pre-Reading
Key framework
Crossing the Chasm · Greiner Growth Curve

Growth is treated as evidence that a venture is working. It is more accurately a change of problem. The practices that produced the first forty customers are usually the practices that prevent the next four hundred, and the failure is structural rather than personal.

Moore's chasm names the market version of this. Early adopters buy a vision and tolerate incompleteness; the early majority buys references, integrations, documentation and support from a vendor that will still exist next year. These are not the same customer with different budgets. They are two populations with incompatible buying logic, and the transition is a discontinuity rather than a slope. Crossing it means narrowing — dominating one beachhead segment with a complete offer — at exactly the moment the team feels ready to broaden.

Greiner (1972) names the organisational version. Each phase of growth is driven by a dominant management practice, and each ends in the crisis that practice produces: founder-led creativity ends in a leadership crisis, direction ends in an autonomy crisis, delegation ends in a control crisis. The solution to one phase creates the problem of the next. Founders who read this as a personal failure delay the structural change; founders who read it as a schedule prepare for it.

Measurement changes too. A scaling venture needs one metric that measures value received by the customer, decomposed into drivers each team can actually move. Activity metrics multiply as headcount grows, and a team optimising its own function while the aggregate stalls is the ordinary consequence of having no shared value measure.

Where AI is part of the product, scaling has a fourth dimension. The adoption research is consistent: pilots fail at integration rather than at accuracy. Data must be maintained, people must be capable of supervising the system, governance must exist before scale rather than after, and the process must be redesigned around the capability rather than have it bolted on. A model that performs well on the data of your first forty customers and is never monitored in production is a liability waiting for its first complaint.

The course closes on the responsibility question, which is also a commercial one: human oversight, robustness, transparency, fairness and a named accountable person. Ventures that can answer these at the point of scaling are the ones still answering them at the point of exit.

Scaling is a set of deliberate decisions — narrow the segment, change the structure, measure delivered value, and govern the system before it grows.

Questions to bring to the session

  1. Which beachhead segment could you dominate entirely within twelve months?
  2. Which growth crisis is closest for your venture, and what structure would answer it?
  3. If your venture deploys AI at scale, who is the named person accountable for its oversight?
