Creative Destruction

Innovation destroys the old economic structure while creating the new one
Slibrary 01
Sheet 1 / 5

Professional definition

The process by which entrepreneurial innovation continuously dismantles established economic structures and replaces them with new ones, making disruption the normal condition of a market economy rather than an exception.

Who developed it

Joseph A. Schumpeter, an Austrian economist teaching at Harvard, named the process in Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy (1942), building on his earlier Theory of Economic Development (1911).

Why it was developed

Neoclassical economics modelled markets as tending towards equilibrium, which left the entrepreneur with nothing to do. Schumpeter needed a mechanism that explained growth as an internal, disruptive force rather than an external shock.

Figure 1 · Creative Destruction

The incumbent structure is not improved but replaced; the curve resets on a new basis.

How it is used

Ask what existing structure your venture would have to destroy for it to succeed, and who owns that structure today. If the answer is nothing, the idea is an improvement, not an innovation.

When it is used

At the opportunity screening stage, and again whenever a venture claims a durable advantage.

By whom it is used

Founders testing the ambition of an idea, and analysts assessing whether an incumbent's position is structurally safe.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

Earlier growth theory treated technology as an exogenous input. Schumpeter made the entrepreneur the endogenous engine of change and reframed profit as a temporary rent that competition necessarily erodes.

Effectuation — Bird-in-Hand

Start from means (who I am, what I know, whom I know), not from goals
Slibrary 01
Sheet 2 / 5

Professional definition

A decision logic used by expert entrepreneurs in which action begins with available means and affordable loss rather than with predicted goals, treating the future as something to be co-created with stakeholders instead of forecast.

Who developed it

Saras D. Sarasvathy, then at Carnegie Mellon and later at Darden, derived the logic from think-aloud protocol studies with 27 expert entrepreneurs, published in 2001.

Why it was developed

Textbook planning assumed a predictable market. Sarasvathy observed that experienced founders did the opposite: they started from who they were, what they knew and whom they knew, and let goals emerge from the commitments they secured.

Figure 2 · Effectuation — Bird-in-Hand

Two decision logics. Expert founders switch to the right-hand column when the future cannot be forecast.

How it is used

Inventory your three means, define the loss you can afford on the next step, and convert interested parties into self-selected stakeholders whose commitments reshape the goal.

When it is used

In genuine Knightian uncertainty, where no reliable market data exists yet.

By whom it is used

First-time founders without capital, and corporate teams entering markets that do not yet exist.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

Prior entrepreneurship research described what successful ventures looked like after the fact. Effectuation described the reasoning process itself and made it teachable, replacing expected return with affordable loss as the decision criterion.

Timmons Model

Opportunity, team and resources held in balance by the entrepreneur
Slibrary 01
Sheet 3 / 5

Professional definition

A venture-creation model in which the entrepreneurial process is driven by the fit and dynamic balance between three forces — the opportunity, the resources and the team — under conditions of ambiguity and uncertainty.

Who developed it

Jeffry A. Timmons of Babson College, with Stephen Spinelli, developed the model across successive editions of New Venture Creation from 1977 onwards.

Why it was developed

Venture failure was routinely attributed to a single cause — a bad idea, a weak team, too little money. Timmons argued that failure comes from imbalance between the three, and that the founder's job is the balancing itself.

Figure 3 · Timmons Model

The entrepreneur holds the three forces in balance; ambiguity and uncertainty act on all three at once.

How it is used

Score the opportunity, the team and the resources separately, identify the weakest of the three, and make the next milestone address that leg rather than the one you enjoy working on.

When it is used

At every funding or hiring decision, and in any go/no-go review.

By whom it is used

Founding teams, incubator mentors and investors screening early-stage ventures.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It displaced the money-first model of venture creation. Timmons insisted the opportunity comes first and that resource parsimony — doing more with less — is a design principle rather than a constraint.

Double Diamond

Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver — two cycles of divergence and convergence
Slibrary 01
Sheet 4 / 5

Professional definition

A four-phase design process alternating divergent and convergent thinking twice: first to find the right problem (Discover, Define) and then to find the right solution (Develop, Deliver).

Who developed it

Published by the UK Design Council in 2005 after studying the design processes of eleven global companies; refreshed as the Framework for Innovation in 2019.

Why it was developed

Design teams consistently converged too early, solving the first problem they recognised. The model makes divergence an explicit, budgeted phase so that the problem is examined before the solution is committed to.

Figure 4 · Double Diamond

Two cycles of divergence and convergence: the first finds the right problem, the second the right solution.

How it is used

Keep the problem open through Discover, close it with a single problem statement at Define, generate multiple solutions in Develop, and commit to one in Deliver.

When it is used

At the start of any venture or product cycle, and again after a pivot.

By whom it is used

Design and product teams, and founders running idea generation workshops.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

Earlier stage-gate models converged monotonically towards a decision. The Double Diamond made divergence a legitimate, scheduled activity and separated problem definition from solution design.

NABC Idea Screen

Need, Approach, Benefit per cost, Competition — a one-page value test
Slibrary 01
Sheet 5 / 5

Professional definition

A four-part screening structure for early ideas that forces an explicit statement of the customer Need, the proposed Approach, the Benefit per cost delivered, and why it beats the Competition and alternatives.

Who developed it

Curtis R. Carlson and William W. Wilmot developed NABC at SRI International, where it became the standard value-proposition discipline; published in Innovation: The Five Disciplines (2006).

Why it was developed

SRI needed a way to kill weak projects early and cheaply. The four questions force a value claim that is specific, quantified and comparative rather than enthusiastic.

Figure 5 · NABC Idea Screen

A one-page value proposition test. An idea that fails any quadrant fails the screen.

How it is used

Write one page: the need with its evidence, the approach, the benefit per cost in numbers, and the competition including the customer doing nothing. Present in two minutes and iterate.

When it is used

In idea screening, before any resources are committed to development.

By whom it is used

Innovation teams, research groups commercialising technology, and students screening venture ideas.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

Unlike a SWOT or a scoring sheet, NABC demands quantification of benefit relative to cost and treats inaction as a competitor, which removes the most common false positive in idea evaluation.

Prompt Ladder

Zero-shot → few-shot → chain-of-thought → role and constraint prompting
Slibrary 02
Sheet 1 / 5

Professional definition

A staged model of prompting practice in which instruction quality is escalated — from a bare request, to worked examples, to explicit reasoning steps, to a defined role, audience and constraint set — to raise the usefulness of model output.

Who developed it

Assembled from the prompting literature: few-shot behaviour was documented by Brown et al. (2020) and explicit reasoning steps by Wei et al. (2022); the ladder framing is the teaching structure used in this course.

Why it was developed

Students treat generative models as search engines and judge them on their weakest possible input. The ladder makes prompt quality a controlled variable so that model output can be evaluated fairly.

Figure 6 · Prompt Ladder

Instruction quality escalates step by step; each rung raises the reliability of the output.

How it is used

Start at the top rung for anything that matters: state the role, the audience, the constraints, give two worked examples, and request the reasoning before the conclusion.

When it is used

Before any AI-supported task in this course, from idea generation to plan critique.

By whom it is used

Every student using AI tools for coursework, and teams standardising internal AI practice.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It replaces prompt folklore with a testable progression: the same task run at each rung produces a measurable difference in quality, which is the exercise run in Unit 1.

Jagged Technological Frontier

AI is excellent at some tasks and quietly terrible at neighbouring ones
Slibrary 02
Sheet 2 / 5

Professional definition

An empirical finding that current AI capability is uneven across tasks of similar apparent difficulty, so performance gains depend on knowing which side of the invisible frontier a given task falls.

Who developed it

Fabrizio Dell'Acqua and colleagues at Harvard Business School, with the Boston Consulting Group, in a randomised controlled field experiment with 758 consultants (2023).

Why it was developed

Aggregate claims about AI productivity concealed the variance. The experiment measured tasks on both sides of the capability boundary and found large gains on one side and significant quality losses on the other.

Figure 7 · Jagged Technological Frontier

Capability is uneven across tasks of similar apparent difficulty; the boundary is not visible from the task.

How it is used

Before delegating a task, test the tool on a version of it where you already know the right answer. Treat unverified output on unfamiliar tasks as a hypothesis, not a result.

When it is used

Whenever a new class of task is delegated to an AI tool for the first time.

By whom it is used

Anyone integrating AI into professional work, and teams writing internal AI usage guidance.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It replaced the general question 'is AI useful?' with the operational one 'is this task inside the frontier?', and documented the failure mode of uncritical acceptance under time pressure.

Prediction-Judgment Split

AI lowers the cost of prediction; the value of human judgement rises
Slibrary 02
Sheet 3 / 5

Professional definition

An economic model of AI in which machines supply cheap prediction while humans supply judgement — the valuation of outcomes and the decision about what to do — making the two complements rather than substitutes.

Who developed it

Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb of the Rotman School, University of Toronto, in Prediction Machines (2018) and its 2022 sequel Power and Prediction.

Why it was developed

Executives were asking whether AI would replace people. The authors reframed AI as a fall in the price of one input, which turns the question into a standard economics problem of substitutes and complements.

Figure 8 · Prediction-Judgment Split

As the price of prediction falls, the value of the complement — judgement — rises.

How it is used

Decompose a venture task into its prediction and its judgement. Delegate the prediction, keep and document the judgement, and price the decision on the judgement, not the forecast.

When it is used

When designing which parts of a workflow to automate, and when defining human oversight roles.

By whom it is used

Founders designing AI-supported operations and managers writing AI governance policy.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It moved AI strategy from technology capability to input economics, giving non-technical decision-makers a usable rule: cheap prediction increases, rather than decreases, the return on good judgement.

EU AI Act Risk Pyramid

Unacceptable, high, limited and minimal risk — four regulatory tiers
Slibrary 02
Sheet 4 / 5

Professional definition

The risk-based classification of the EU Artificial Intelligence Act, which assigns obligations to AI systems in four tiers according to the risk they pose, from prohibited practices to minimal-risk applications.

Who developed it

The European Parliament and Council, in Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the first comprehensive statutory framework for AI in any jurisdiction.

Why it was developed

Sectoral product law could not address systems whose risk depends on use rather than on the product itself. A horizontal, risk-tiered regulation places duties in proportion to potential harm to health, safety and fundamental rights.

Figure 9 · EU AI Act Risk Pyramid

Obligations scale with risk. Most start-up applications sit in the lower two tiers but must still be classified.

How it is used

Classify each AI use in the venture against the four tiers, document the classification, and check whether you act as provider, deployer or both — the duties differ.

When it is used

Before deploying any AI system in a product, and whenever the purpose of an existing system changes.

By whom it is used

Founders shipping AI features in the EU market, and anyone deploying AI in recruitment, credit, education or safety contexts.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

Earlier AI governance was voluntary and principle-based. The Act converts principles into enforceable obligations with a defined classification method and penalties, and binds deployers as well as developers.

Human-in-the-Loop Ideation

Generate, critique, correct, re-prompt — with a human at every gate
Slibrary 02
Sheet 5 / 5

Professional definition

An interaction pattern in which AI-generated options are systematically reviewed, corrected and approved by a person before use, creating a controlled loop in which authorship and accountability remain human.

Who developed it

Formalised in human-AI interaction research, notably the guidelines of Amershi et al. (2019) at Microsoft Research, synthesising twenty years of interaction design findings.

Why it was developed

Systems that produce output without a review gate transfer errors directly into decisions. The loop makes the review point explicit and turns each correction into the next instruction.

Figure 10 · Human-in-the-Loop Ideation

The loop keeps authorship and accountability with the person at every gate.

How it is used

Never accept the first output. Review against stated criteria, correct, re-prompt with the correction, and record which decisions were yours.

When it is used

In every AI-supported task in this course, and mandatorily in anything submitted for assessment.

By whom it is used

Students, and any team deploying AI in decisions with consequences for people.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It converts responsible AI from a value statement into an operating procedure with a defined checkpoint, which is what makes the AI declaration in this course auditable.

Customer Development Model

Customer discovery, validation, creation and company building
Slibrary 03
Sheet 1 / 6

Professional definition

A four-step process that runs in parallel to product development, testing the business model's hypotheses with real customers before scaling, and treating a start-up as a temporary organisation searching for a repeatable model.

Who developed it

Steve Blank, after eight Silicon Valley ventures, published the process in The Four Steps to the Epiphany (2005) and refined it with Bob Dorf in 2012.

Why it was developed

Start-ups were being managed with the tools of established companies, which assume a known business model. Blank separated the search for a model from the execution of one, and built a process for the search.

Figure 11 · Customer Development Model

Search precedes execution: the first two steps test the model, the last two scale it.

How it is used

Convert each business-model assumption into a testable hypothesis, leave the building to test it with customers, and only enter customer creation once the sales model repeats.

When it is used

From the first week of a venture until product-market fit is evidenced.

By whom it is used

Founders, corporate innovation teams, and every student building the Assessment 1 validation report.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It reversed the sequence: product development had been the primary process with customers consulted at launch. Blank made customer learning the primary process and product a consequence of it.

TAM-SAM-SOM

Total, serviceable and obtainable market — sized from the bottom up
Slibrary 03
Sheet 2 / 6

Professional definition

A three-level market sizing structure distinguishing the total addressable market, the share of it a business model can serve, and the share it can realistically capture within the planning horizon.

Who developed it

Standard practice in venture finance, formalised for entrepreneurs in John Mullins's The New Business Road Test, which sets out the bottom-up market sizing discipline.

Why it was developed

Founders routinely claimed a fraction of an enormous market as their forecast. The three-level structure exposes the difference between a market that exists and a market a specific venture can reach.

Figure 12 · TAM-SAM-SOM

Each level removes what your model, geography, channel and capacity cannot actually serve.

How it is used

Build SOM bottom-up from channels, capacity, conversion rate and price. Derive TAM afterwards for context, never the other way around.

When it is used

In the market analysis section of both assessments, and in any investor conversation.

By whom it is used

Founders preparing financial projections and investors testing them.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It replaced the single 'market size' figure with a chain of explicit exclusions, so that each reduction can be challenged on its own evidence.

Jobs to Be Done

Customers hire a product to make progress in a situation
Slibrary 03
Sheet 3 / 6

Professional definition

A theory of demand holding that customers do not buy products but hire them to accomplish a job — a progress they are trying to make in a particular circumstance, with functional, social and emotional dimensions.

Who developed it

Developed by Clayton M. Christensen with Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon and David Duncan (Competing Against Luck, 2016), drawing on earlier work by Anthony Ulwick and Rick Pedi.

Why it was developed

Correlational segmentation — age, income, industry — predicted who bought but never why. Christensen wanted a causal theory of demand that could tell a firm what to build.

Figure 13 · Jobs to Be Done

The customer hires a product to make progress in a specific circumstance.

How it is used

Interview for the circumstance: what was happening when you looked for a solution, what did you use before, what would have made you not switch? Define the job, then design against it.

When it is used

In customer discovery and again when defining the value proposition.

By whom it is used

Product teams, founders writing personas, and anyone whose competitors are not who they expected.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It shifted the unit of analysis from the customer to the job, which reveals non-obvious competitors — including the customer's existing workaround and doing nothing at all.

Empathy Map

Says, thinks, does, feels — plus pains and gains
Slibrary 03
Sheet 4 / 6

Professional definition

A visual synthesis tool that organises observed and reported customer data into what a person says, thinks, does and feels, together with their pains and gains, to build a shared and evidenced view of the user.

Who developed it

Created by Dave Gray of XPLANE and popularised through Gamestorming (2010); later integrated into the Strategyzer customer profile.

Why it was developed

Interview notes were being summarised into flattering generalities. The map forces raw data into six distinct buckets, where the gap between what a person says and what they do becomes obvious.

Figure 14 · Empathy Map

Observed and reported data are separated so that contradictions become visible.

How it is used

Fill only with evidence from actual interviews or observation. Mark each entry with its source. Contradictions between quadrants are the most valuable findings.

When it is used

Immediately after each customer interview, before memory reconstructs the conversation.

By whom it is used

Discovery teams and students building the customer profile for Assessment 1.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

Unlike a persona, which invents a plausible individual, the empathy map contains only observed material and makes the absence of evidence visible as an empty quadrant.

The Mom Test

Ask about their life and past behaviour, never about your idea
Slibrary 03
Sheet 5 / 6

Professional definition

An interviewing discipline that avoids compliments and hypothetical commitment by asking only about the interviewee's past behaviour, real problems and actual spending, so that the data collected is not distorted by politeness.

Who developed it

Rob Fitzpatrick, a founder who had raised money on the strength of false-positive interviews, published the discipline in 2013.

Why it was developed

People protect the feelings of the person asking. Any question about a hypothetical future or about your idea returns encouragement rather than information, which is worse than no data at all.

Figure 15 · The Mom Test

Every branch avoids the hypothetical; only the past and a commitment produce usable data.

How it is used

Ask what they did last time, what it cost them, and what they have already tried. Close by asking for a commitment of time, reputation or money — not for an opinion.

When it is used

In every customer interview, from the first conversation onwards.

By whom it is used

Founders, product managers and every student collecting validation evidence.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It made interview quality a property of the questions rather than of the interviewer's skill, and defined a falsifiable success criterion: a commitment, not a compliment.

Ten-Step Positioning Process

Position derived from evidence, not written as a slogan
Slibrary 03
Sheet 6 / 6

Professional definition

A structured method that derives a product's market position from the alternatives customers would otherwise choose, the attributes only this product holds, the value those attributes enable and the segment that cares about it disproportionately, expressed inside a market category chosen deliberately rather than inherited.

Who developed it

April Dunford, after two decades of product marketing and sixteen product launches in B2B technology companies, published the process in 2019 having found that positioning was being handled as a copywriting task.

Why it was developed

Teams argued about wording with no evidence base and defaulted to the category they had been founded in, even where that category made their value invisible. Ries and Trout had established what a position is; no one had specified how to arrive at one.

Figure 16 · Ten-Step Positioning Process

Dunford's ten steps group into six moves; the category is chosen last, once the evidence is in.

How it is used

Work the steps in order: list the true competitive alternatives including the manual workaround, isolate the attributes only you hold, map each attribute to the value it enables, identify the customers who care disproportionately about that value, and only then choose the market category in which the value is obvious. Layer a trend on only if it is genuinely relevant.

When it is used

After customer discovery and competitive analysis, before the unique selling proposition, the marketing plan and the pitch.

By whom it is used

Founders, product marketers, and every student writing the USP in the Assessment 1 validation report.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

Ries and Trout established that a position is a place in the customer's mind. Dunford made it an output of evidence rather than of copywriting, and made the market category itself a variable to be chosen rather than an assumption to be inherited.

Porter's Five Forces

Rivalry, buyers, suppliers, entrants and substitutes shape profitability
Slibrary 04
Sheet 1 / 5

Professional definition

An industry analysis framework identifying five competitive forces — rivalry among existing firms, buyer power, supplier power, threat of new entrants and threat of substitutes — that determine structural profitability.

Who developed it

Michael E. Porter of Harvard Business School, translating industrial organisation economics into a managerial tool in a 1979 Harvard Business Review article and Competitive Strategy (1980).

Why it was developed

Strategy teaching relied on case-by-case judgement with no theory of why some industries were systematically more profitable than others. Porter supplied the structural explanation.

Figure 17 · Porter's Five Forces

Structural profitability is set by five forces before any individual firm makes a move.

How it is used

Assess each force as high, medium or low with evidence, then ask which force your venture changes and whether that change is defensible.

When it is used

In the competitive analysis of Assessment 1 and before entering any new market.

By whom it is used

Founders, strategists and investors assessing structural attractiveness.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It moved competitive analysis from a list of named rivals to the structure of the industry, showing that the most dangerous competitor is often not a firm at all but a substitute or a supplier.

Strategy Canvas & ERRC Grid

Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create — a differentiated value curve
Slibrary 04
Sheet 2 / 5

Professional definition

A pair of tools plotting how an industry competes on each factor of value and then reconfiguring that profile by eliminating, reducing, raising and creating factors to open uncontested market space.

Who developed it

W. Chan Kim and Renee Mauborgne of INSEAD, from an analysis of 150 strategic moves across 30 industries over more than a century, published as Blue Ocean Strategy (2005).

Why it was developed

Competitive strategy assumed a fixed market to be fought over. The authors documented cases where firms created new demand instead, and needed a tool that made the trade-off between value and cost visible and breakable.

Figure 18 · Strategy Canvas & ERRC Grid

The left column lowers cost, the right column raises value; value innovation requires both.

How it is used

Plot the industry's competing factors as a value curve, then apply the four actions. A profile identical to the industry average is a warning, not a plan.

When it is used

When positioning a new venture, and whenever differentiation is asserted but not demonstrated.

By whom it is used

Founders defining a position, and teams whose offer looks like everyone else's.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

Porter presented differentiation and cost leadership as alternatives. The ERRC grid operationalises pursuing both by removing factors the industry competes on but customers do not value.

Positioning Map

Two decision attributes that matter, plotted against rivals
Slibrary 04
Sheet 3 / 5

Professional definition

A two-dimensional map placing competing offers against the attributes customers actually use to choose, in order to locate a defensible and communicable position rather than an arbitrary gap.

Who developed it

Al Ries and Jack Trout introduced positioning in a 1972 Advertising Age series and in Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind (1981); perceptual mapping came from marketing research practice.

Why it was developed

Advertising had focused on the product's qualities. Ries and Trout argued that the scarce resource is space in the customer's mind, and that a position must be claimed relative to what is already there.

Figure 19 · Positioning Map

Axes must be the criteria customers actually use to choose, not attributes convenient to plot.

How it is used

Choose two axes that are genuine buying criteria, plot every alternative including the incumbent workaround, and state in one sentence the position you will defend.

When it is used

After competitive analysis, before writing the USP and the marketing plan.

By whom it is used

Founders, brand managers and students defining a unique selling proposition.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It shifted the object of analysis from the product to the customer's mental map, and made the empty space on the map a hypothesis to be tested rather than an opportunity to be assumed.

Retrieval-Grounded Research Loop

Retrieve sources first, then generate — never the reverse
Slibrary 04
Sheet 4 / 5

Professional definition

A research pattern in which a language model is grounded in retrieved documents before it generates, so that every statement can be traced to a retrievable source rather than to model memory.

Who developed it

The retrieval-augmented generation architecture was introduced by Patrick Lewis and colleagues at Facebook AI Research in 2020; the research discipline built on it is the working protocol of this course.

Why it was developed

A language model generating from parametric memory produces fluent statements with no retrievable origin. Grounding the generation in retrieved documents makes the origin checkable, which is the minimum condition for academic use.

Figure 20 · Retrieval-Grounded Research Loop

Retrieval precedes generation, so every statement can be traced back to a retrievable document.

How it is used

Search first, collect sources, then ask the model to summarise only from those sources. Open every source it names before using the claim.

When it is used

In all desk research for the market analysis and the literature sections of both assessments.

By whom it is used

Students conducting market research, and any professional using AI for evidence-based work.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It converts the model from an oracle into an interface over a document set, which is the difference between an AI-assisted literature review and an unciteable summary.

Source Triangulation Check

Three independent sources before a figure becomes a fact
Slibrary 04
Sheet 5 / 5

Professional definition

A verification discipline requiring corroboration from independent sources — and explicit flagging of single-source, vendor-commissioned or self-reported figures — before data is presented as established fact.

Who developed it

Triangulation comes from social science methodology (Denzin, 1978); its application to AI-generated content responds to the hallucination literature surveyed by Ji et al. (2023).

Why it was developed

Generative models produce false statements with the same fluency as true ones, and vendor-commissioned market data has a direction of interest. Neither problem is solved by reading more carefully; both require independent corroboration.

Figure 21 · Source Triangulation Check

A figure becomes a fact only where independent sources agree; single-source data carries a visible caveat.

How it is used

For any load-bearing figure, find three independent sources. Flag vendor and self-reported data. Delete claims that cannot be corroborated rather than softening them.

When it is used

Before any number enters a submission, a pitch or a financial model.

By whom it is used

Every student in this course; the check is part of the marking criteria for market analysis.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It applies an established methodological safeguard to a new failure mode, and sets a clear removal rule — unverifiable claims are cut, not hedged.

Business Model Canvas

Nine building blocks describing how an organisation creates and captures value
Slibrary 05
Sheet 1 / 5

Professional definition

A shared visual language describing a business model through nine interlocking blocks — customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships and cost structure.

Who developed it

Alexander Osterwalder, from his 2004 doctoral thesis at HEC Lausanne supervised by Yves Pigneur, developed with a community of 470 practitioners and published in 2010.

Why it was developed

Business models were discussed without a shared vocabulary, so two people could agree on the words and disagree on the model. The canvas gave the concept a fixed ontology that could be drawn, compared and argued over.

Figure 22 · Business Model Canvas

Nine blocks in one page: the right side creates value, the left side delivers it, and the two bottom blocks close the economics.

How it is used

Fill all nine blocks on one page in bullet form, then read it as a sentence: for this segment, we solve this, delivered this way, paid for like this, at this cost.

When it is used

In Assessment 1, and at every material change to the venture thereafter.

By whom it is used

Founders, corporate innovation teams and investors comparing models across ventures.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

Earlier business-model writing was descriptive prose. The canvas made the model a manipulable object: blocks can be changed one at a time and the consequences traced across the page.

Value Proposition Canvas

Customer jobs, pains and gains matched to products, relievers and creators
Slibrary 05
Sheet 2 / 5

Professional definition

A detailing tool for two canvas blocks that tests fit between a customer profile — jobs, pains and gains — and a value map of products and services, pain relievers and gain creators.

Who developed it

Alexander Osterwalder, Yves Pigneur, Greg Bernarda and Alan Smith, in Value Proposition Design (2014), detailing two blocks of the original canvas.

Why it was developed

Teams filled the value proposition block with adjectives. The canvas splits the claim into a customer profile built from evidence and a value map built from the offer, so that fit becomes testable.

Figure 23 · Value Proposition Canvas

Two halves tested against each other; the overlap is evidence, not intention.

How it is used

Rank the customer's jobs, pains and gains by importance from interview data. Map only the relievers that address the top-ranked items. Ignore the rest.

When it is used

Immediately after customer discovery, before product decisions are made.

By whom it is used

Product teams and students completing the value proposition block of Assessment 1.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It introduced ranking: not every pain deserves a reliever, and a proposition that addresses low-ranked pains looks complete on paper while failing in the market.

Problem, solution, key metrics and unfair advantage for early ventures
Slibrary 05
Sheet 3 / 5

Professional definition

A start-up adaptation of the Business Model Canvas that replaces four blocks with problem, solution, key metrics and unfair advantage, prioritising risk and uncertainty over description.

Who developed it

Ash Maurya, founder of Spark59 and LEANSTACK, adapted the Business Model Canvas in Running Lean (2010, second edition 2012).

Why it was developed

The original canvas assumed a describable business. Maurya's ventures had none: what they had was risk. He replaced the descriptive blocks with the ones that capture what could kill the venture first.

Figure 24 · Lean Canvas

Four blocks of the original canvas are replaced by problem, solution, key metrics and unfair advantage.

How it is used

Complete it in twenty minutes, mark the riskiest assumption, and design the next week's experiment against that assumption alone.

When it is used

Weekly, as the working document between class sessions.

By whom it is used

Early-stage founders and student teams iterating on a venture idea.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It made the canvas a risk instrument rather than a description, and introduced the single key metric so that a team cannot claim progress on four fronts at once.

Slibrary 05

Professional definition

A structured set of recurring patterns by which value is monetised, each with distinct implications for cash timing, customer commitment, churn exposure and forecasting method.

Consolidated from the business-model literature, principally Osterwalder and Pigneur's revenue stream typology and subsequent platform and subscription research.

Why it was developed

Founders chose a price and called it a model. The taxonomy separates the mechanism of payment from the amount, because the mechanism determines cash timing, customer commitment and the shape of the forecast.

Select the pattern, then derive the forecast method it requires: subscription needs churn and cohort data, usage needs volume drivers, freemium needs a conversion rate and a marketing cost.

In the revenue streams block, before any financial projection is built.

By whom it is used

Founders building the financial plan and anyone pricing a new offer.

It links the revenue block of the canvas directly to the forecasting method in the financial plan, which is where student business plans most often become internally inconsistent.

Unit Economics: CAC, LTV, Payback

What one customer costs, what one customer is worth, how fast you recover
Slibrary 05
Sheet 5 / 5

Professional definition

The per-customer economics of a business model, comparing the fully loaded cost of acquiring a customer with the contribution margin that customer generates over their lifetime, and the time taken to recover the acquisition cost.

Who developed it

Formalised in software venture practice, notably by David Skok (Matrix Partners) in the SaaS Metrics series, building on customer-equity work by Blattberg, Getz and Thomas.

Why it was developed

Ventures with growing revenue were failing because each new customer cost more than they contributed. Per-customer economics exposes that before the aggregate numbers do.

Figure 26 · Unit Economics: CAC, LTV, Payback

One customer, three numbers. The relationship between them decides whether growth creates or destroys value.

How it is used

Compute CAC from all sales and marketing spend, LTV from contribution margin and retention, and payback in months. Treat an LTV:CAC below three as a model problem, not a marketing problem.

When it is used

In the financial plan of Assessment 2, and before any decision to increase marketing spend.

By whom it is used

Founders, growth teams and investors evaluating the quality of growth.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It reframed marketing spend as an investment with a measurable payback period, which converts a cost debate into a capital allocation decision.

Data Network Effects Flywheel

More users create more data, which improves the product, which attracts users
Slibrary 06
Sheet 1 / 5

Professional definition

A self-reinforcing loop in which usage generates data that improves algorithmic performance, which improves the value of the offer and attracts further usage — the central defensibility mechanism of data-driven ventures.

Who developed it

Theorised by Robert Gregory, Ola Henfridsson, Evgeny Kaganer and Harris Kyriakou in the Academy of Management Review (2021), formalising a mechanism observed across data-centric firms.

Why it was developed

Data was described as an asset without a mechanism explaining how it produced advantage. The paper specified the loop and the conditions under which it actually turns.

Figure 27 · Data Network Effects Flywheel

The loop only closes if the improvement is visible to the user; otherwise data accumulates without returning value.

How it is used

Identify which user action generates the training data, what the model improves, and how the user experiences that improvement. Missing any link means there is no flywheel.

When it is used

When claiming defensibility in a pitch, and when designing product telemetry.

By whom it is used

Founders of data-driven ventures and investors testing moat claims.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It distinguished data network effects from classical network effects: value comes from the algorithmic improvement returned to the user, not from the number of other users.

Slibrary 06

Recurring configurations by which firms monetise machine intelligence — selling it as a service, embedding it in an existing product, or rebuilding the operating model around a data and algorithm core.

Marco Iansiti and Karim R. Lakhani of Harvard Business School, from field research on data-centric firms including Ant Financial, Amazon and Ocado, published in Competing in the Age of AI (2020).

Firms were adding AI features to unchanged operating models and seeing no strategic effect. The authors showed that the advantage comes from rebuilding the operating model around a data and algorithm core.

Ask whether AI improves your existing value proposition, is itself the product, or replaces the way work is done. Only the third removes the constraint between scale and headcount.

When designing the business model and when assessing whether an AI claim is substantive.

By whom it is used

Founders positioning an AI venture, and reviewers of AI-labelled business plans.

It relocated AI strategy from the product to the operating model, and explained why AI-centric firms scale without the diseconomies that limit traditional operating models.

Strategic value against internal capability decides the sourcing route
Slibrary 06
Sheet 3 / 5

Professional definition

A sourcing decision matrix plotting the strategic value of an AI capability against the firm's ability to build and maintain it, to determine whether to develop in-house, licence, or partner.

Who developed it

A sourcing application of the make-or-buy literature to AI capability, set out for AI specifically by Iansiti and Lakhani (2020) and widely used in enterprise AI practice.

Why it was developed

Early ventures spent scarce engineering capacity rebuilding commodity capabilities. The matrix separates what must be owned because it is the advantage from what should be bought because it is infrastructure.

Figure 29 · Build, Buy or Partner for AI

Strategic value on one axis, honest capability on the other; most founders overestimate the second.

How it is used

Place each AI capability on both axes with evidence for the capability claim, then commit: build only where the capability is real and the value is strategic.

When it is used

In the operational plan, and at every technology procurement decision.

By whom it is used

Technical founders and non-technical founders receiving build recommendations.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It applies a classic sourcing decision to a capability whose maintenance cost, not build cost, dominates — which is why partnering is more often correct than founders expect.

AI Cost Iceberg

Licences are visible; data, integration, monitoring and rework are not
Slibrary 06
Sheet 4 / 5

Professional definition

A total-cost model showing that the visible subscription or inference cost of an AI capability is a small fraction of its full cost, which includes data preparation, integration, monitoring, retraining, compliance and human review.

Who developed it

Grounded in the machine-learning systems literature, in particular the technical debt analysis by D. Sculley and colleagues at Google (2015), which quantified the maintenance burden of ML in production.

Why it was developed

Business cases for AI were built on the subscription price. The research showed that model code is a small fraction of a production system and that surrounding infrastructure dominates the lifetime cost.

Figure 30 · AI Cost Iceberg

The licence line is the smallest part of the total cost of an AI capability in production.

How it is used

Cost every layer of the iceberg in the financial plan, including a permanent line for human review. If a layer is zero, state why.

When it is used

In the cost structure block and in the financial plan of Assessment 2.

By whom it is used

Founders costing AI features and reviewers assessing whether an AI business case is credible.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It moved the AI cost conversation from price per token to total cost of ownership, which is where most AI business cases fail after deployment rather than before.

Professional definition

Business readers do not read to the end. Minto's insight was that the writer's discovery order and the reader's comprehension order are opposites, and the document must follow the reader's.

In the executive summary, the pitch opening and every recommendation you make.

Anyone writing for a decision-maker, and every student presenting in Week 10.

It replaced narrative build-up with answer-first structure, and made logical grouping — the MECE test — a checkable property of a document rather than a matter of taste.

A high proportion of AI pilots never reached production. The research found the causes were organisational — process, governance and capability — rather than model performance.

Pilots fail at integration, not accuracy; all four conditions must hold before scaling.

How it is used

Score all four conditions before scaling. Where process redesign has not happened, expect the pilot's benefits not to appear at scale.

When it is used

At the transition from pilot to production, and in the scaling section of the business plan.

By whom it is used

Founders scaling an AI-enabled operation and teams reviewing AI investment cases.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It relocated the failure point of AI projects from the technology to the operating model, which is why upskilling consistently outperforms replacement in adoption studies.

Professional definition

The seven requirements for trustworthy AI set out by the European Commission's High-Level Expert Group, used here as the closing responsibility checklist for any venture deploying AI at scale.

Who developed it

The European Commission's High-Level Expert Group on AI, comprising 52 experts, published the guidelines in 2019 and the ALTAI assessment list in 2020.

Why it was developed

Ethical principles for AI were abundant and unusable. The group translated them into seven requirements with a practical self-assessment, which later informed the AI Act.

Figure 81 · Trustworthy AI Requirements

Seven requirements, assessed through the ALTAI self-assessment list; accountability requires a named person.

How it is used

Run the seven requirements as a closing checklist before any AI deployment scales, and name the person accountable for each.

When it is used

At the end of the course as the responsibility review, and before any AI product launch.

By whom it is used

Founders deploying AI and teams preparing for regulatory obligations.

Innovation relative to the predecessor tool

It bridged principles and law: the requirements are the direct ancestor of the obligations later made binding by the EU AI Act.
